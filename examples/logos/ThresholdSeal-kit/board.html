The Promise Office · one mark, considered

The Threshold Seal

A ring that is not quite closed, and a small amber arc that closes it. The gap is the work; the amber is the moment it gets done.

Why this, and not a finished circle

A seal has to be earned before it can close.

Every consulting logo reaches for a finished circle — a badge, a stamp, something that already looks complete. That is the wrong picture for what you actually sell. A client hires you before the loop is closed, while the promise is still open. So the mark shows the true state of most engagements: nearly there, held together by one deliberate, differently-coloured piece. The plum arc is the 80% any capable team can carry. The amber arc is the last 20% — the part that needs someone who has closed this loop before.

01

The open ring

Drawn, never printed as a static badge. It is caught mid-close, so the mark reads as an act, not an object.

02

The amber arc

Not a dot, not decoration — it is sized and placed to be the one piece that would complete the ring if it were finished. That is the whole idea in one shape.

03

The built P

Drawn from a stem and a half-ring bowl, not traced from a typeface. The stem is what a client hands you; the bowl is what you shape around it.

How it is constructed

Three parts, geometrically exact.

The promiseA ring carried 300° of the way round — the bulk of any engagement.
The deliveryThe remaining 60° — smaller, but the only part the ring needs to be whole.
The officeBoth pieces, plus the P, held to one geometry — the Threshold Seal.
